Funding

Small-Business Funding Options Compared: Bootstrapping, Loans, Grants and Investors

By the 556678 Editorial Team · 8 min read · Updated November 2026

An honest comparison of the main ways to fund a small business, with the real costs, trade-offs and who each option suits.

Money is a tool, and every type of funding has a price — paid in interest, ownership, time or control. The right choice depends on how fast you need to grow, how predictable your cash flow is and how much control you want to keep.

1. Bootstrapping (revenue and savings)

Cost: slower growth and personal financial risk. Upside: full ownership and discipline. Personal savings remain the most common source of startup capital. Bootstrapping suits service businesses and products that can sell before they are perfect. Pre-sales, deposits and annual plans are bootstrapping's best friends.

2. Friends and family

Flexible and fast, but relationships are on the line. Put everything in writing — amount, whether it's a loan or equity, repayment terms — and only accept money people can afford to lose.

3. Term loans and lines of credit

Cost: interest and fees; usually requires time in business, revenue and sometimes collateral or a personal guarantee. Upside: you keep ownership. Government-backed loan programs and banks typically offer the lowest rates; online lenders are faster but often more expensive. Compare offers on APR and total cost with our loan calculator.

4. Microloans and community lenders

Smaller amounts, often paired with mentoring, and more open to newer businesses or owners with thin credit histories. Community development lenders and nonprofit programs are worth checking in most regions.

5. Grants and pitch competitions

Cost: time spent applying. Upside: non-dilutive money and publicity. Grants are competitive and usually tied to specific goals — innovation, export, hiring, sustainability or under-represented founders. Pitch competitions run by governments, banks, telecoms and business associations award cash and in-kind prizes. Treat them as a bonus, not a plan, and reuse your application materials across several.

6. Revenue-based financing

Repayment as a percentage of monthly revenue until a fixed cap is reached. It flexes with your sales, which helps seasonal businesses, but the effective cost can be high. Read the cap carefully.

7. Angel investors and venture capital

Cost: ownership and expectations of very fast growth. Suited to businesses that can plausibly become very large. Most small businesses are not — and that is perfectly fine. If you go this route, learn how to pitch clearly and know your unit economics cold.

Choosing: three questions

  1. Can the business repay debt from predictable cash flow? If yes, debt is usually cheaper than equity.
  2. Is speed worth giving up ownership? Only if being first truly decides the market.
  3. What happens in the bad case? Personal guarantees and high-cost advances can follow you if the business fails.
Funding amplifies whatever is already true. If the unit economics work, money speeds you up. If they don't, money makes the losses bigger.
