Break-Even Analysis in Plain English: The First Number Every Founder Needs

By the 556678 Editorial Team · 6 min read · Updated November 2026

How to calculate your break-even point, why contribution margin matters, and how to use break-even to set prices and goals.

Before you can grow, you need to know where the floor is. The break-even point tells you how much you need to sell to cover every cost of running the business. Below it you lose money; above it every additional sale contributes to profit. It is the most practical number in small-business finance, and it takes five minutes to calculate.

Fixed costs vs variable costs

Fixed costs stay roughly the same whether you sell one unit or a thousand: rent, salaries, insurance, software, loan payments. Variable costs rise with each sale: materials, packaging, shipping, card fees, sales commissions. Some costs are semi-variable — a phone plan with overage charges, for example. Put them where they mostly behave.

Contribution margin is the engine

Price minus variable cost per unit is your contribution margin. It is the slice of each sale that is available to pay fixed costs. If you sell a candle for $30 and it costs $11 in wax, jar, wick, packaging and fees, each sale contributes $19.

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin per unit. With $3,800 of monthly fixed costs, that candle business needs 200 sales a month to break even.

Using break-even to make decisions

Testing a price change

Raise the candle to $34 and contribution jumps to $23; break-even falls to about 166 units. Would you lose more than 34 sales a month from the price increase? Often not — which is why small price increases are usually worth testing.

Evaluating a new hire

A $4,000-a-month hire adds $4,000 of fixed costs. Divide by contribution margin and you know exactly how many extra sales the hire needs to generate (or enable) to pay for themselves.

Setting sales goals

Add your target profit to fixed costs before dividing. The result is a concrete monthly sales goal that connects directly to what you want to earn.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting your own salary in fixed costs, which makes the business look healthier than it is.
  • Leaving out payment and marketplace fees from variable costs.
  • Using an average price when your sales mix is changing quickly.
  • Calculating once and never again — costs move every year.
