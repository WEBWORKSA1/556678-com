Pricing for Profit: A Small-Business Playbook

By the 556678 Editorial Team · 7 min read · Updated November 2026

Cost-plus, competitor and value-based pricing explained, plus tiering, anchoring and how to raise prices without losing customers.

Pricing is the fastest profit lever most businesses never pull. A cost reduction takes months to find and negotiate; a price change can be live by this afternoon. Yet many owners set a price once, based on a guess, and leave it untouched for years.

The three pricing lenses

1. Cost-plus: the floor

Add up the full cost of delivering one unit — including fees and a share of your time — and add your target margin. This tells you the minimum you can charge without losing money. It never tells you the maximum.

2. Competitor pricing: the context

Customers compare. Knowing where competitors sit helps you position deliberately: cheaper and simpler, matched but better served, or premium with a clear reason. Don't simply copy — their costs and goals aren't yours.

3. Value-based pricing: the ceiling

What is the result worth to the customer? A bookkeeping service that saves a café owner ten hours a month and avoids tax penalties is worth more than the bookkeeper's hourly cost. Pricing against outcomes is how small firms escape price wars.

Packaging beats discounting

  • Good / better / best tiers let customers self-select and make the middle option feel like the smart choice.
  • Bundles raise average order value without training customers to wait for sales.
  • Annual plans with a modest discount bring cash forward and reduce churn.
  • Add-ons (rush delivery, premium support, setup) capture value from customers who want more.

How to raise prices

  1. Start with new customers; grandfather existing ones for a period if relationships are important.
  2. Give notice — 30 days is typical — and explain what has improved or what costs have risen.
  3. Pair the increase with something new: a feature, faster turnaround, a better guarantee.
  4. Track conversion and churn for 60 days. If nothing moves, you were underpriced.
If nobody ever pushes back on your price, it is probably too low.

Psychology that helps (when used honestly)

Anchoring — showing a higher-priced option first — makes the main option look reasonable. Charm pricing ($49 rather than $50) can lift conversions for consumer products. Clear guarantees reduce perceived risk. Use these to help customers decide, never to mislead them.
