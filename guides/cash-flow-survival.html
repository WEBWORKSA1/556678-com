Cash flow

Why Profitable Businesses Still Run Out of Cash (and How to Stop It)

By the 556678 Editorial Team · 8 min read · Updated November 2026

Cash flow, not profit, decides whether a small business survives. A practical system for forecasting, collecting faster and building a buffer.

Ask a room of business owners whether they are profitable and most will say yes. Ask how many weeks of expenses they could cover if sales stopped tomorrow and the room goes quiet. That silence is the gap between profit and cash — and it is where a large share of small businesses get into trouble. Cash-flow problems are among the most frequently cited reasons for small-business closures, ahead of competition and product issues.

Profit and cash are measured differently

Profit is an accounting result: revenue earned minus expenses incurred in a period. Cash is what is in the bank. The two drift apart for predictable reasons:

  • Customers pay late. You booked the sale in March; the money arrives in May.
  • Inventory eats cash early. You buy stock months before you sell it.
  • Loan principal is not an expense. It doesn't reduce profit, but it leaves the account every month.
  • Taxes arrive in lumps. Quarterly or annual payments can wipe out a "good" month.
  • Growth costs money first. Hiring, equipment and marketing are paid before the new revenue lands.

The faster you grow, the wider the gap can get. That is the paradox: the businesses with the most demand are often the ones that run short.

The 13-week cash forecast

The single most useful habit is a rolling 13-week cash forecast. It takes under an hour to set up in a spreadsheet:

  1. Start with today's bank balance.
  2. List expected cash in each week — not invoices sent, but money you realistically expect to receive, based on how your customers actually pay.
  3. List cash out each week: payroll, rent, suppliers, loan payments, tax instalments, subscriptions.
  4. Calculate the closing balance for each week and highlight any week that dips below your safety floor.
  5. Update it every Monday. Compare forecast with actual so you get better at predicting.

A forecast turns cash problems from surprises into scheduling problems — and scheduling problems can be solved weeks in advance. Pair it with our cash runway calculator for the longer view.

Collect faster

  • Invoice immediately on delivery, not at month end.
  • Shorten terms. Net 15 instead of Net 30 is often accepted without discussion.
  • Take deposits for custom work and larger projects — 30% to 50% up front is common.
  • Make paying easy with card and bank-transfer links on every invoice.
  • Automate reminders at 3 days before due, on the due date and 7 days after.

Pay smarter, not later

Stretching suppliers damages relationships, but you can align outflows with inflows: negotiate payment dates that fall after your main collection days, move annual subscriptions to monthly while cash is tight, and buy inventory in smaller, more frequent batches when the unit-cost difference is small.

Build the buffer before you need it

Set a cash floor — for example, one month of fixed costs — and treat it as untouchable. Transfer a fixed percentage of every deposit into a separate reserve account until you reach three to six months of expenses. Apply for a line of credit while the business looks strong; lenders are far more generous to companies that do not urgently need the money.

The 5-6-7-8 cash rhythm: forecast on Monday (5), invoice the same day you deliver (6), chase anything 7 days overdue, and keep 8 weeks of fixed costs as your minimum floor while you build toward six months.

Warning signs to act on

  • You check the bank balance before approving routine expenses.
  • Receivables are growing faster than revenue.
  • You are using a credit card to cover payroll.
  • Your forecast keeps being too optimistic.

Any one of these is a signal to tighten collections, pause discretionary spending and talk to your accountant or a business mentor. Our free growth plan request is a good place to start if you want a second pair of eyes.
